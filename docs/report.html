Wardrobe Muse

Five open-weight model setups played stylist with the same 69-item wardrobe and 12 briefs, from a law-firm interview to “dress as the feeling of a Sunday evening”. Every outfit is a set of item IDs, so creativity and rule-following can be measured exactly, and the stated reasoning can be tested against the actual choice.

3,840 outfits generated2,534 valid4 temperatures × 2 prompt orders × 8 samples220 planted-item trialsvLLM on one L40S via Modal

What we found

  1. Thinking made Qwen3 reliable and more faithful, not more original.With thinking on, Qwen3-8B produced the most valid outfits (94%), met the most constraints (80%) and got the best judge fit (7.21). Its explanations named items it didn't wear only 4.5% of the time, against 18–61% for the others. It also made the most predictable choices: lowest novelty, 1.87 bits per item.
  2. Qwen2.5-7B was the most creative by our definition.Counting outfits that were valid, met the brief's constraints, scored fit ≥ 7 and were more unusual than the median for their brief, Qwen2.5-7B hit 27.6%, against 16.3% for both Qwen3 setups.
  3. Reasoning before choosing raised creativity in models without thinking.Qwen2.5-7B's creative hit rate went from 19.5% to 35.7%. Qwen3-8B went from 8.9% to 23.7%, though its valid outfits fell from 96% to 71%. With thinking on, the order made no difference.
  4. Models mostly defended an item they never picked.When one item in their own outfit was secretly swapped for an off-brief piece, models defended it in 67–94% of trials. Only Qwen3-8B with thinking flagged it noticeably more often than an unaltered item: 7/24 versus 1/24. Qwen2.5-7B flagged it 2/24 times.
  5. Higher temperature bought novelty at the cost of validity for small models.Phi-4-mini went from 69% valid at T=0.3 to 7% at T=1.3. Qwen3-8B stayed between 82% and 85%.
  6. The judge's “originality” score and statistical rarity barely agree.Spearman ρ between surprisal and judged originality was 0.07. Rarer outfits were judged to fit slightly worse (ρ = -0.28), which is the novelty-versus-appropriateness trade-off creativity research describes.

The planted-item test

Each model saw one of its own outfits with a single piece swapped for an item sharing no style tags with the rest, then was asked to explain the outfit item by item and say if anything didn't belong.

Second judge

The first judge was Qwen3-8B, which shares a family with two contestants. To check for self-preference, every valid outfit and every planted-item explanation was re-scored by IBM Granite-3.3-8B, which comes from a family none of the contestants belong to. Granite scores about one point more generously, so the bias column compares z-scored ratings. A positive value means the Qwen3 judge liked that model's outfits more than Granite did.

Method and limits

Setup

  • Models: Qwen3-8B (thinking on and off), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Phi-4-mini-instruct, OLMo-2-1124-7B-Instruct, served with vLLM 0.9.2 on one L40S via Modal.
  • 12 briefs: 3 conventional, 4 with constraints (colour, warmth, required tie, rugged footwear), 5 open-ended.
  • Judges: Qwen3-8B with thinking off, re-checked by IBM Granite-3.3-8B; both at temperature 0 and blind to which model made the outfit. Qwen3-8B also served as the reconstruction reader.

Limits

  • Both judges are 8B models, and they agree only moderately on single outfits. Human ratings on a sample would show which judge tracks taste better.
  • Novelty is relative to these five setups, not to human stylists.
  • Redaction removes item names and IDs, but paraphrases like “the red scarf” still leak, so reconstruction scores run high for every model.
  • Planted-item results rest on 17–24 trials per setup; treat differences of a few trials as noise.
